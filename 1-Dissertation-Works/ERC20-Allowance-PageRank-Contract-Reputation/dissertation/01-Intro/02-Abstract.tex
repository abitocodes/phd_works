\chapter*{Abstract}

\label{ch:abstract}

Users of decentralized finance (DeFi) let smart contracts move their tokens. Before an owner trades through a router or deposits into a vault, the owner signs an ERC-20 approval for that contract and has to judge whether the contract deserves this trust. Reputation scores computed on transaction graphs could support that judgement. An existing method, Adaptive Weighted PageRank (AWP), ranks addresses on token transfers. A transfer records a payment, whereas an allowance records that an owner authorized a spender to move its tokens. This thesis asks what ERC-20 allowance edges add to PageRank as a reputation score for smart contracts on Arbitrum One, compared with the transfer walk and with the raw degrees that both walks smooth.

The study defined EndorseRank, PageRank on the latest non-zero allowances with AWP's edge weights and uniform restarts, which ranks the spender contracts that owners approve. It also defined a coupled PageRank (C-PR) that walks both edge types in one chain. An analysis plan that the study committed on 9~October~2026, before it extracted any log, fixed the cohorts, scores, labels, statistics and the decision rule for C-PR. From public Arbitrum One logs of October~2023 to June~2026, the study selected the $27{,}844$ contracts that at least three distinct owners had approved; externally owned accounts entered the graphs only as owners, senders and recipients. The study compared every score and two raw degrees through Kendall's $\tau_b$ with paired bootstrap 95\% intervals, in the same window and on two holdouts. In W0 the study froze the scores on 31~March~2026 and counted the labels from April to June. In W1 it froze them on 30~June~2026 and counted the labels from July to September, under a rule that it registered before extracting those logs.

EndorseRank and AWP each agreed most with the checks built from their own edges, as intended and partly by construction: EndorseRank reached $\tau=0.481$ $[0.476, 0.487]$ on the allowance checks and AWP $\tau=0.447$ $[0.440, 0.453]$ on the transfer checks. The two scores ordered the contracts differently ($\tau=0.217$ $[0.209, 0.225]$). Out of window, AWP ranked later new transfer senders better than the raw transfer in-degree in W0 and W1 ($\Delta\tau=+0.038$ $[0.035, 0.042]$ and $+0.020$ $[0.016, 0.023]$). EndorseRank, with the uniform restarts that the plan fixed, ranked later new approval pairs below the raw count of approving owners ($\Delta\tau=-0.011$ $[-0.016, -0.006]$ and $-0.029$ $[-0.032, -0.025]$), although the allowance graph did carry propagation beyond one hop. Neither the plan nor the registration fixed these comparisons with the raw degrees in advance; the study computed them after the labels were known. A variant of EndorseRank with AWP's activity restarts beat the count in both windows, but the study chose to look at that variant against the count only after seeing the labels, so the result is a hypothesis for a registered test, not a finding. C-PR at $\lambda=0.5$ met neither its planned nor its registered rule: in W1 it beat the transfer walk on new approval pairs ($+0.101$ $[0.092, 0.111]$) but failed the registered margin of $0.01$ on new senders ($-0.025$ $[-0.029, -0.021]$) and on the liquidation-free close rate of GMX~V2 contract traders ($-0.044$ $[-0.100, -0.002]$). On that outcome, which GMX closes record and neither graph contains, the registered comparison found no count-level lead of allowance over transfer in W1 ($\Delta\tau=+0.063$, 97.5\% interval $[-0.040, 0.158]$, $70$ traders), and the PageRank versions are not resolved. EndorseRank's graph had $0.37$ times AWP's edges ($22.3$ against $59.7$ million), and its mean solve time was $0.42$ times AWP's ($29.6$ against $69.8$ seconds); the difference in time follows from the number of edges, not from the algorithm.

Compared with existing techniques, EndorseRank gives an ordering of contracts that the transfer-based AWP does not, and it ranks a contract by the approvals that other addresses give it, whereas deployed attestation tools score the holder of an address. It did not beat the approval count that any protocol can compute without PageRank, whereas AWP did beat its own count. The thesis contributes the allowance as a reputation edge for contracts and an out-of-window test of each score against the count it smooths. It also reports that one walk over both edge types failed the rules fixed for it in advance.

For the people who approve contracts, the number of distinct owners that approve a contract is a public signal that wallet software could show before a user signs, and EndorseRank, as planned, did not improve on it out of window. A Sybil model of EndorseRank on the same graph shows how cheaply such signals move: $13$ colluding addresses lift a contract to the cohort's 90th percentile, about $1{,}250$ to the 99th and about $1.08$ million to the top. Looking ordinary is cheap and reaching the top is expensive, so no score studied here should decide warnings or rewards on its own, and none says whether a contract's code is safe.

\emph{Key terms:} decentralized finance; smart contract; on-chain reputation; ERC-20 allowance; PageRank; EndorseRank; Adaptive Weighted PageRank; coupled PageRank; temporal holdout; Arbitrum One; Kendall's tau
